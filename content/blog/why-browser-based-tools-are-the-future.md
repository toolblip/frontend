There's a quiet revolution happening in developer tooling. The app you used to download, install, and update every few weeks? It might not exist in five years — replaced by something that loads in 200 milliseconds and asks nothing of your system.

**Privacy-first by default**

When you run a Base64 encoder locally in your browser, the data never leaves your machine. No server, no logs, no "we promise we don't look at your JSON." This isn't just a nice-to-have — for anyone handling API keys, tokens, or sensitive payloads, it's the difference between "I think this is safe" and "I know it is."

Compare that to the web app you paste the same payload into. Now your data is on someone else's server, potentially in someone else's logs. The browser-level sandbox changes the entire trust model.

**Zero friction, infinite reach**

Download a desktop app and you're committing to: finding the right version for your OS, running an installer, dealing with auto-updates, and praying it doesn't conflict with your existing tools. Or — click a link, and 300ms later you're working.

This matters especially for tools you use infrequently. You probably don't need a cron expression parser installed permanently on your machine. But you'll need one eventually, at 2am, on a new machine. Browser tools scale down to zero and up to whatever you need, with nothing in between.

**Speed that desktop can't match**

Modern browsers are incredibly fast. V8, SpiderMonkey, and the rest have had years of optimization poured into them. For most developer utilities — JSON formatting, regex testing, hash generation — the browser runs the code as fast as any native binary, with zero startup time.

**The real win: collaboration**

Sharing a browser tool is sharing a URL. No Docker containers, no "works on my machine" problems, no version mismatches. Send someone a link to a formatted request, and they see exactly what you see.
