Every time you download a new app to format some JSON or resize an image, you're making a small trade-off. You're trusting a file you don't know that well, giving it disk space, and hoping it doesn't phone home with anything weird.

Browser-based tools flip that model entirely.

## Your Data Stays With You

When a tool runs in your browser, the processing happens on your machine. No server round-trip. No uploads. Your sensitive JSON, your Base64 strings, your images — none of it leaves your device unless you explicitly send it somewhere. That's not just a convenience; it's a genuine privacy win.

Compare that to dozens of small SaaS utilities that quietly log everything you paste into them. You don't always know what happens to that data.

## No Install, No Update, No Hassle

Open a tab, paste your content, done. There's no "download installer.dmg" with a progress bar, no version 3.1.2 to hunt for, no "restart required." Browser tools are always the latest version by default. They're also cross-platform by nature — your tool works the same on a Chromebook, a Linux workstation, or a borrowed Windows machine.

For teams, this is even better. Nobody's on an outdated version because they forgot to update. There's nothing to provision or license either.

## Speed That Native Apps Can't Beat

This one surprises people. Yes, really — browser tools can be faster for many tasks. You're not waiting for an app to cold-start, navigate directories, or load its UI framework. The browser already has your context. Paste, click, copy. Done in under five seconds.

For the occasional task — which covers most developer tooling needs 80% of the time — this speed compounds. You're not context-switching away from your terminal or IDE.

## The Trade-offs Are Shrinking

Yes, browser tools have historically been limited. But WebAssembly, better JS APIs, and browser extensions have closed most of the gap. Heavy image processing, complex regex evaluation, even terminal emulation — all in the tab.
