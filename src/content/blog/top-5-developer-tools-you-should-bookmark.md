Every developer has a mental list of small utilities they use constantly. The ones that solve those awkward one-off problems — formatting some messy JSON, decoding a Base64 string, figuring out what a cron expression actually means. Usually these live in browser tabs you keep open, or bookmark bars full of random sites you've accumulated over the years.

Here's the thing: most of those tools are bloated, slow, or full of ads. Here are five worth actually keeping.

## 1. JSON Formatter & Validator

If you've ever pasted JSON into an error message to figure out what went wrong, you know this pain. A good JSON formatter takes messy, minified, or invalid JSON and gives you clean, readable output — with clear error messages when something's broken. It should also validate and let you know exactly which character caused the problem.

## 2. Base64 Encoder / Decoder

Base64 shows up constantly in APIs, tokens, and data URIs. A quick encode/decode tool that handles text, files, and URLs without fuss is genuinely useful. The browser-only kind means you're not sending your data anywhere, which matters when you're working with anything sensitive.

## 3. Regex Tester & Debugger

Writing regex is one thing. Testing it is another. A solid regex tester lets you throw in your pattern, paste some sample text, and see matches highlighted in real time. Bonus points if it explains what each part of your pattern does — because you'll forget by Tuesday.

## 4. Cron Expression Parser

Cron expressions are notoriously hard to read. `*/15 9-17 * * 1-5` — is that business hours? Weekdays? Both? A cron parser that translates the expression into human-readable language and shows you the next N execution times is one of those tools you didn't know you needed until you use it once and wonder how you lived without it.

## 5. Hash Generator (MD5, SHA-1, SHA-256, etc.)

Generating hashes comes up more than you'd expect — checking file integrity, verifying passwords in dev environments, working with APIs that sign requests. A tool that computes MD5, SHA-1, SHA-256, SHA-512, and more — from text or files, all client-side — handles this without making you crack open OpenSSL docs.

## The Common Thread

All five of these are small, focused utilities. They do one thing well. They work in your browser without installing anything. And they handle sensitive data locally, so nothing gets logged or sent to some analytics service you didn't know existed.
