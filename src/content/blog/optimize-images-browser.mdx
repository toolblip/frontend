You need to resize an image. Your instinct might be to upload it to some website, wait for a server to process it, and download the result. Here's a better way: do it all in your browser. No upload. No server. No waiting.

## Why Local Image Processing Matters

When you upload an image to a third-party site, you're trusting that company with your data. Even if they have good intentions, their servers become a target. Data breaches happen. Privacy policies change. You might accidentally upload something sensitive.

Browser-based image processing eliminates this entirely. Your image never leaves your device. The browser — through Canvas API, FileReader, and increasingly WebAssembly — can handle most image tasks locally with excellent performance.

## Image Cropping Without an Editor

Need to quickly crop an image to a specific aspect ratio? A browser-based cropper lets you drag, set exact dimensions, and download the result in seconds. No Photoshop. No GIMP. No opening a heavy application at all.

For social media assets, profile pictures, or blog thumbnails, a dedicated cropper tool is faster than opening Figma for something this simple.

## Format Conversion in Seconds

JPEG to PNG. PNG to WebP. HEIC to JPEG (for the iPhone users who keep sending you HEIC files). Browser-based converters handle all of this without a server round-trip.

WebP and AVIF offer dramatically better compression than legacy formats, but not every tool supports them natively. A quick browser-based conversion gives you the modern format you need without migrating your entire workflow.

## Compression Without Quality Loss (Well, Controlled Loss)

Image compression tools let you dial in the quality/size tradeoff precisely. Need a screenshot under 100KB for a README? Set your target size, get a preview, and download. Going from 4MB to 400KB takes seconds and is indistinguishable at web resolutions.

## Privacy by Design

The most compelling argument for browser-based image tools isn't speed or convenience — it's privacy. Your images are yours. They stay on your machine, in your browser, processed by your CPU/GPU. There's no "we may retain your uploaded images for improvement purposes" fine print.
